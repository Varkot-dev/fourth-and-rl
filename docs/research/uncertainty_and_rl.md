# Uncertainty and RL: research design for M5, M6 and M7

Written 2026-11-17. Notation: W(a) is the true win probability of action a in a given state, What(a) its estimate, g = W(go) - W(kick) the true gain, ghat its estimate, SE its standard error. "Points" means percentage points of win probability. All numeric examples are illustrations computed for this note (closed form, checked by Monte Carlo), not results.

## 0. The plan's framing is flawed, and the corrected version

The plan compares "maximize the point estimate" with "act only when the ensemble agrees". Three problems:

1. **There is no "not acting".** Punting is an action. "Act only when confident" really means "follow a default action when unsure", so the rule is only as good as its default. Its justification must be an argument that the default is probably better, not an appeal to uncertainty.
2. **Uncertainty alone never changes the best action.** If the goal is P(win) and beliefs about the model are a posterior, expected win probability of action a is E[W(a) | data]. The Bayes action maximizes the posterior mean. The spread of the posterior does not enter.
3. **Risk aversion has no room here.** The outcome is binary, so any utility over {win, lose} is a linear function of P(win). Risk aversion over points (what Sandholtz et al. infer for coaches) is a different objective from winning. The only coherent "caution" left is about model uncertainty (ambiguity), which is a choice of objective and must be defended as one.

**Corrected question.** Given a finite, selectively collected log of coach decisions, (i) how much win probability does each decision rule actually lose relative to the true optimum, as a function of data size and of how conservative the logging policy was, and (ii) how much can we honestly claim about the size of coaches' errors? Uncertainty matters through six separate channels (section 1), and the simulator with a known exact solution lets us measure each.

## 1. When does uncertainty matter? Six cases

**(a) Pure noise, symmetric stakes: the decision is unchanged, the claim is not.** ghat = +1.0 point, SE = 2.0, flat prior. Posterior mean of g is +1.0, so go. P(go is truly better) = Phi(0.5) = 0.69, which the humility paper would label "lean". A rule that kicks whenever agreement is below 83% gives up 1.0 point in expectation each time. The 83% category is the right tool for the statement "the coach was wrong", and the wrong tool for choosing the action.

**(b) Optimizer's curse (Smith and Winkler 2006).** True values equal: W(go) = W(kick) = 0.50. SE is 0.04 for go (rarely tried) and 0.01 for kick, errors independent. Plug-in picks the larger estimate.
- E[max] = 0.50 + sqrt(0.04^2 + 0.01^2) / sqrt(2 pi) = 0.516: the chosen action's value is overstated by 1.6 points on average.
- When go is chosen, its estimate averages 0.531 (overstated 3.1 points); when kick is chosen, 0.502 (0.2 points). The average claimed edge of a "go" recommendation is 3.3 points when the true edge is zero.
- With a third action (punt, SE 0.01, same truth), go is chosen 45% of the time, not 33%. The noisiest action wins most often.
- If go is truly 2 points worse, plug-in still picks it 31% of the time at SE 0.04, against 8% if its SE were 0.01.

The cure is shrinkage (posterior means), and it has two consequences people confuse. With a prior on g centered at zero (sd 2 points), ghat = +3 and SE = 4 gives posterior mean 3 * 4 / (4 + 16) = +0.6: the claim shrinks fivefold, the action does not change. Shrinkage toward zero never flips a two-action decision. It flips only if the prior mean favors the default (prior mean -1 gives 0.2 * 3 + 0.8 * (-1) = -0.2, kick), or with three or more actions and unequal SEs: estimates go 0.52 (SE 0.04) and field goal 0.51 (SE 0.01), common prior 0.50 (sd 0.02), give posterior means 0.504 and 0.508, so field goal. So "follow the default when unsure" is Bayes only under an informative prior that coaches are roughly right. That is an empirical assumption we can test, not a consequence of humility.

**(c) Asymmetric coverage.** Kicks and punts have much more data, so SE(go) is larger, which feeds (b). Worse, go data is selected: coaches go when unobserved factors favor it (fourth and inches recorded as fourth and 1, a mismatch). That is bias, not variance, and the bootstrap cannot see it (the humility paper says so in its Section 5). This is the one place where discounting the rarely tried action has a real justification: the estimate is biased upward by an unknown amount.

**(d) Repeated decisions.** Suppose g is +2 or -2 with equal probability. One decision: indifferent. With many future decisions, trying go until the sign is known is worth 0.5 * 2 = 1 point per future decision. Exploration value favors the under-sampled action, the opposite of the "abstain" rule. A rule that never goes when unsure never gathers data and stays unsure.

**(e) Robust and pessimistic objectives.** Maximin over a confidence set (robust MDPs) and lower-confidence-bound rules (pessimism, CQL) are not Bayes for P(win). Using go 0.52 (SE 0.04) and field goal 0.51 (SE 0.01), a one-SE lower bound gives 0.48 and 0.50, so field goal. Legitimate justifications: (1) misspecification or confounding outside the posterior, as in (c); (2) frequentist guarantees without a prior: pessimism bounds regret by the uncertainty along the optimal policy only (Jin, Yang and Wang 2021), and is near optimal when the logging policy is near expert (Rashidinejad et al. 2021); (3) an adversary who adapts. Counterweight: Xiao et al. (2021) show that in batch bandits greedy, pessimistic and optimistic index rules are all minimax optimal and none dominates instance by instance. Pessimism is a bet that the logging policy is good. If coaches are too conservative, pessimism inherits their mistake.

**(f) Evaluating coaches.** The usual estimate, sum over plays of max_a What(a) - What(coach's action), is nonnegative by construction. If the coach is exactly optimal and the SE of the difference is 4 points, the expected "loss" is 4 * 0.399 = 1.6 points per play, about 1.6 phantom wins over 100 fourth downs. Errors are shared across plays through one model, so it does not average away. Counting only confident plays selects on a large ghat / SE, another winner's curse. An honest number needs sample splitting (recommend from one set of games, value on another) and an interval whose coverage has been checked.

## 2. Literature

Verification: arXiv IDs below were checked on 2026-11-17 (the abstract at each ID matches the description). Journal details for non-arXiv items are from memory and marked unverified; the papers themselves were not re-read.

- **Optimizer's curse.** Smith and Winkler, "The Optimizer's Curse", Management Science 2006 (volume unverified). Choosing the maximum of unbiased estimates gives a positively biased value; Bayesian shrinkage removes it. The RL version is maximization bias in Q-learning (van Hasselt, Guez and Silver, arXiv 1509.06461).
- **Robust MDPs.** Iyengar, "Robust Dynamic Programming", and Nilim and El Ghaoui, "Robust Control of Markov Decision Processes with Uncertain Transition Matrices", both 2005 (Mathematics of Operations Research and Operations Research; unverified). Maximin value iteration is tractable when the uncertainty set is rectangular (chosen independently per state-action). Rectangularity lets nature be worst everywhere at once, which is overly conservative, and a bootstrap ensemble is not rectangular.
- **Posterior sampling.** Osband, Russo and Van Roy (arXiv 1306.0940): sample one MDP from the posterior, act optimally for it, repeat. Bootstrapped DQN (Osband et al., arXiv 1602.04621) approximates this with an ensemble. Both are exploration methods; for a one-shot decision the posterior mean is the right rule.
- **Offline RL.** Fitted Q iteration (Ernst, Geurts and Wehenkel, JMLR 2005; unverified). BCQ (Fujimoto, Meger and Precup, arXiv 1812.02900): restrict actions to those the data supports; the original is for continuous control. CQL (Kumar et al., arXiv 2006.04779): a regularizer pushes down Q on actions outside the data so the learned Q lower-bounds the policy's value. Pessimism theory: Jin, Yang and Wang (arXiv 2012.15085); Rashidinejad et al. (arXiv 2103.12021); Xiao et al. (arXiv 2104.02293). Tutorial: Levine et al. (arXiv 2005.01643).
- **Off-policy evaluation.** Importance sampling is unbiased but its variance explodes when the target policy takes actions coaches rarely take. Doubly robust (Jiang and Li, arXiv 1511.03722) combines a model with importance weights. Fitted Q evaluation (Le, Voloshin and Yue, arXiv 1903.08738). All assume no unobserved confounding.
- **Deep ensembles** (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, arXiv 1612.01474): seed-to-seed spread, which is not sampling variance.
- **Conformal prediction** (tutorial: Angelopoulos and Bates, arXiv 2107.07511). Not relevant here: it covers observable outcomes (win or loss), not the unobserved probability, and assumes exchangeability that within-game plays violate.

## 3. Experiments

**Common setup.** Truth M* is the fitted full-game MDP from M4. The opponent is fixed to the coach-like policy, so our side faces a single-agent MDP with exact Q*, V* and pi* by backward induction. The behavior policy mu_k is the M6 behavior-cloning model of real coaches with k added to the logit of "go" (k = 0 is real coaches, negative is more conservative). A dataset is N simulated games under mu_k, N in {250, 1000, 4000, 16000} (4000 is about our real sample), R = 50 seeded replicates. Fitting uses the same pipeline as on real data. Every learned policy is scored by exact policy evaluation in M*: regret = V*(kickoff) - V^pi(kickoff), in points per game.

Two worlds. **World A (well specified):** the fitting family contains the truth. **World B (confounded):** each fourth down has an unlogged i.i.d. variable z (think inches to go) that changes conversion probability and that the coach sees. Because z is i.i.d., the best z-blind policy is still an exact DP on the marginal model, so truth stays known, but logged go attempts overstate conversion.

**E1. Optimizer's curse.** Question: does the plug-in rule overstate the value of what it picks, and more so for go? Setup: fit M_hat, solve it, get pi_hat and Qhat. Per state compare Qhat(s, pi_hat(s)) with the true value of that action followed by pi_hat in M*. Vary N and k. Metrics: mean overstatement by chosen action and by log-count of (s, a); slope of true gain on claimed gain over the coach's action. Expected: positive overstatement, largest for go and thin cells, shrinking like 1 / sqrt(N); slope below 1; larger in World B. Figure: claimed versus true gain by recommended action with the 45-degree line; overstatement versus count.

**E2. Does bootstrap agreement mean what it says?** Among state-replicate pairs with agreement p, how often is the recommended action truly optimal? Compare the game bootstrap, the fractional bootstrap (phi from 0.35 to 1) and a deep ensemble. Expected: overconfident in World A (the Brill simulation found 0.71 coverage at nominal 0.90 for the cluster bootstrap in a simpler game), badly so in World B. Brill et al. propose tuning phi on synthetic seasons from a realistic simulator; this is that test. Figure: reliability curve of agreement versus true correctness.

**E3. Decision rules by true regret.** Rules: plug-in; bagged mean over replicates; empirical-Bayes shrinkage of action gains (prior variance estimated across states); abstain (plug-in if agreement >= t, else the behavior policy's modal action, t swept from 50% to 100%, 83% marked); pessimistic (argmax of the 5th or 25th bootstrap percentile of Q); robust DP over rectangular bootstrap intervals. References: mu_k itself and pi*. Vary N, k, world. Expected: in World A, plug-in, bagged and shrinkage are close and beat abstain except at small N, with a crossover N* that grows as coaches get closer to optimal; abstain has a regret floor from the true gains it forfeits; in World B pessimistic rules may win because they offset the upward bias of go. Figure: regret versus N per rule, and a map of the best rule over (N, k) with a marker at (4000, 0).

**E4. Offline RL with known truth.** Algorithms on logged transitions only: behavior cloning, fitted Q iteration, discrete CQL over a sweep of its penalty weight (d3rlpy; current API to be checked), and the model-based plug-in from E3. Vary k, an epsilon of random actions mixed into mu, and N. Metrics: true regret of each greedy policy; Q overestimation by visit count. Expected: FQI overestimates rare go actions and goes too often in thin states; CQL removes that and moves toward behavior cloning, helping when mu is near optimal and hurting when mu is very conservative; the model-based rule beats all three because it pools conversion data across field position and from third downs. If so, the gap in this problem is less about missing (state, action) support than about selection. No CQL weight may be chosen using true regret; show the whole sweep and what fitted Q evaluation would have picked. Figure: regret versus k per algorithm; count heatmap beside Q error.

**E5. Coach evaluation interval.** The true win probability given up by mu_k, V^pi* - V^mu_k, is known, and one arm sets mu = pi* (true loss zero). Estimators: naive sum over all plays; confident plays only; cross-fitted (recommend from fold A, value with fold B); shrinkage-corrected. Each with a game-bootstrap 90% interval. Metrics: bias, coverage, width versus N. Expected: naive is biased upward and positive even for the optimal coach; nominal 90% intervals undercover; cross-fitting is roughly unbiased for a lower bound. Then run the cross-fitted estimator on real data and report the league number and team rankings with intervals. Figure: sampling distribution of each estimator against the true line; real team intervals.

**E6 (stretch). Learning league.** Each season's policy is a rule applied to all data so far, and the data comes from that policy. Compare plug-in, abstain and posterior sampling (act on one bootstrap replicate) by cumulative regret over 20 seasons. Expected: abstain can lock in conservatism. Figure: cumulative regret curves.

## 4. Candidate headlines, as falsifiable claims

1. **"At today's data size, deferring to coaches when the bootstrap disagrees loses more than it saves."** Confirmed if plug-in (or shrinkage) regret is below abstain regret at N = 4000, k = 0 in World A, with intervals over replicates excluding zero. Refuted if abstain wins there. That refutation is a real finding too: coaches' defaults carry information the model lacks, and the humility categories work as a decision rule, not only as a reporting rule. Report whichever happens, and whether World B reverses it.
2. **"Fourth-down models overstate the gain from going for it, by an amount we can measure."** Confirmed if the E1 slope is clearly below 1 and overstatement is larger for go than for kicks. Refuted if the slope is near 1 at N = 4000.
3. **"The win probability coaches give up is real but much less certain than published tallies suggest."** Confirmed if in E5 the naive estimator is biased upward and undercovers while the cross-fitted real-data interval excludes zero but is wide. Refuted if the naive estimator is nearly unbiased with nominal coverage.

## 5. Pitfalls and guards

- **The simulator is not football.** Every E1 to E6 number is a statement about M*. Say so in the README; only the E5 real-data estimate is about the NFL. Report M4's simulator validation next to these results.
- **World A flatters us.** Fitting the true family removes misspecification. Always show World B, and state that real misspecification is unknown.
- **World B is our invention.** The confounding strength is a free parameter. Sweep it and report at what strength conclusions flip; do not claim it matches reality.
- **The default drives the abstain result.** Report abstain with at least two defaults (coach modal action, always kick or punt).
- **Tuning on the truth.** Thresholds, percentiles and CQL weights must be fixed in config beforehand or shown as full sweeps.
- **Fixed opponent.** Regret is against a best response to coach-like opponents, not an equilibrium. State it.
- **Bootstrap as posterior.** Agreement is not a calibrated probability; E2 measures the gap, and no rule should be described as Bayesian unless a prior is written down.
- **Replicate noise.** Report regret with intervals over the R replicates, with recorded seeds, and state the smallest difference the design can detect.
- **Overclaiming novelty.** The optimizer's curse, pessimism and the bootstrap categories are known. The contribution is measuring them in one football decision problem with known truth; describe it that way.
